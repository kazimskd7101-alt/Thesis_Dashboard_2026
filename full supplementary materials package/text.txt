Abstract—Intraday futures markets generate many noisy shorthorizon price windows, making always-on directional prediction
difficult to use responsibly in trading analytics. This study
develops a selective, explainable decision-support artifact for
MOEX Si futures using candlestick chart images constructed
from fifteen-minute data. Building on the chart-image learning
framework of Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu’s “(Re-)Imag(in)ing Price
Trends,” the study adapts convolutional neural networks to an
intraday futures setting, evaluates longer and multi-scale input
windows, compares CNN outputs with numeric, momentum, HOG,
and HAAR-style baselines, and integrates Grad-CAM visual
diagnostics into a human-in-the-loop dashboard called Decision
Support Studio. The empirical package contains 775,621 raw oneminute rows, 89,628 fifteen-minute by-contract candles, 29,926
clean continuous-front rows, and 502 clean trading dates. The
best full-sample CNN achieves 0.587 accuracy, 0.559 balanced
accuracy, 0.126 MCC, and 0.580 ROC-AUC, indicating only a
modest full-sample edge. Stronger evidence appears under selective
abstention: the strict I120 finalist achieves 0.833 accuracy at 2.0%
coverage, corresponding to 54 selected rows, with 0.834 balanced
accuracy, 0.830 F1-score, and 0.667 MCC. Grad-CAM inspection
suggests chart-anchored but spatially diffuse attention, supporting
visual audit rather than causal proof of candle-level reasoning.
The contribution is a selective, auditable workflow for analyst
inspection, not an automated trading system.
Index Terms—Intraday decision support, candlestick chart
images, convolutional neural networks, Grad-CAM, explainable AI,
futures markets, selective prediction, MOEX Si futures, financial
machine learning.

I. Introduction
Intraday futures trading decisions are made under conditions
of high noise, short feedback cycles and substantial uncertainty.
A trader or analyst observes a continuous stream of price
changes, but only a small fraction of chart windows are likely
to contain sufficiently strong directional evidence to justify
focused attention. Conventional machine-learning systems often
convert this environment into a simple classification task: for
every rolling window, predict whether the future return will
be positive or non-positive. Such an approach is technically
convenient, but it is not necessarily aligned with the practical
decision problem. In a noisy intraday market, a weak prediction
for every window may be less useful than a smaller set of
high-confidence cases that can be inspected, explained and
governed.

This thesis develops an explainable selective decision-support
artifact for intraday MOEX Si futures analysis. The system
adapts the chart-image forecasting logic of Jiang, Kelly and
Xiu’s “(Re-)Imag(in)ing Price Trends” [1] to a fifteen-minute
futures setting. It represents recent Si futures price histories
as candlestick or chart images, trains CNN-based directional
models, applies selective probability-tail rules to abstain from
ambiguous windows, audits selected cases with Grad-CAM
visualizations, and presents the result in a human-in-the-loop
dashboard called Decision Support Studio. The proposed
artifact is not an automated trading system. It is a selective,
auditable workflow for analyst inspection.
The central argument is deliberately cautious. The fullsample forecasting edge is modest. The best full-sample CNN in
the revised experiment, single_I60_ma0_vol0, achieves
0.587 accuracy, 0.559 balanced accuracy, 0.126 MCC and 0.580
ROC-AUC. This result improves on the initial 20-bar baseline,
but it does not justify a universal forecasting claim. The stronger
result appears when the system is used selectively. The strictfinalist model, single_I120_ma0_vol0, achieves 0.833
accuracy at 2.0% coverage, corresponding to 54 selected rows,
with 0.834 balanced accuracy, 0.830 F1-score and 0.667 MCC.
The practical contribution is therefore not that every intraday
window can be predicted reliably. The contribution is that
a selective model-dashboard artifact can abstain from most
ambiguous cases and surface a small set of stronger signals
for human review.

E. Research Questions
The study is guided by three research questions:
RQ1: Can chronology-aware candlestick-chart CNN models
provide useful full-sample directional information
for intraday MOEX Si futures when contract rolls,
intraday session structure and benchmark comparisons
are handled conservatively?
RQ2: Does selective probability-tail abstention improve the
practical decision-support value of the forecasting
artifact compared with compulsory full-coverage
prediction?
RQ3: Can Grad-CAM visual diagnostics and dashboardbased case inspection make selected CNN signals
auditable for human analysts while avoiding strong
claims of precise candle-level or causal interpretability?
These questions preserve the thesis’s cautious framing: the
aim is not to prove automated profitability, but to evaluate
whether selected, visually auditable cases can support analyst
inspection.
F. Object and Subject of the Study
The object of the study is the intraday trading-analytics
decision environment for MOEX Si futures, represented by the
task of interpreting rolling fifteen-minute price histories and
deciding which cases merit analyst attention.
The subject of the study is the design and evaluation of
an explainable selective decision-support system that uses
candlestick chart images, CNN probability estimates, selective

probability-tail rules, Grad-CAM diagnostics and dashboardbased case inspection to support intraday directional analysis.
This distinction is important. The thesis is not only about
a model architecture. It is about the use of a model inside a
practical decision-support workflow.
G. Research Methods
The thesis uses a mixed project-research methodology. The
empirical component includes data preprocessing, continuousfront construction, chart-image generation, supervised learning,
baseline comparison and performance evaluation. The modeling
component includes CNN classifiers, multi-scale input designs,
numeric/logistic baselines, momentum baselines, HOG features
and HAAR-like features. The evaluation component uses
accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, MCC,
ROC-AUC where available, coverage and selected row counts.
The explainability component uses Grad-CAM visual diagnostics. Grad-CAM outputs are analyzed through heatmaponly images, full overlays, masked overlays and raw chart
comparisons. The project component uses design-science logic
to build and evaluate the Decision Support Studio dashboard
as an artifact for analyst inspection. The final interpretation
combines quantitative performance evidence with qualitative
and visual case-level audit.

I. Academic Novelty
The thesis does not claim novelty in inventing CNNs, GradCAM or chart-image learning. Its novelty lies in the adaptation
and integration of these components for a specific intraday
futures decision-support problem. The main novelty elements
are:
1) adaptation of Re-Imag-style chart-image forecasting to
intraday MOEX Si futures;
2) use of multi-scale intraday input windows and selective
probability-tail rules for decision support;
3) comparison of CNN probabilities with numeric, momentum, HOG and HAAR-style baselines in the same project
package;
4) Grad-CAM audit of selected high-confidence cases with
raw, heatmap, overlay and masked views;
5) implementation of a dashboard artifact that operationalizes selective, explainable and human-in-the-loop intraday
decision support.
The novelty is therefore primarily methodological and
artifact-based: the thesis integrates existing ideas into a reproducible, domain-specific decision-support workflow and
evaluates that workflow with actual Si futures data.
J. Practical Value
The practical value of the thesis is the Decision Support
Studio artifact and its associated operating procedure. The
dashboard transforms model outputs into a filtered analyst
queue. It allows the user to inspect raw chart images, model
probabilities, selected signal direction, confidence buckets,
Grad-CAM heatmaps, full overlays, masked overlays and
aggregate performance panels. This helps prevent the model
from being interpreted as a black-box trading command.
For a trading analytics team, the artifact demonstrates
how machine-learning signals can be governed. It shows that
selective coverage should be visible, that XAI should be used
for diagnostic review rather than as proof of correctness, and
that ambiguous windows should be abstained from rather than
forced into recommendations. This has practical relevance even
though the thesis does not claim final transaction-cost-adjusted
profitability.

II. Analysis of Best Practices and Academic Literature
This chapter positions the project within two connected
bodies of knowledge. The first is academic research on financial
machine learning, chart-image forecasting, intraday market
prediction, time-series representation and explainable artificial
intelligence. The second is the practical decision-support
tradition in which analytical systems are designed not only to
produce predictions, but also to organize evidence, support user
judgment, and control the risk of over-reliance on automated
outputs.
The review is structured as a taxonomy rather than as a
chronological list of papers, following the logic of systematic
review guidelines while remaining focused on the project
problem [25], [26]. This is important for the project-based
nature of the thesis. The practical problem is not simply to
train a CNN. The practical problem is to design a selective,
auditable decision-support artifact for intraday trading analysis.
Therefore, the literature is reviewed through six themes:
financial machine learning and chart-image prediction; intraday
and high-frequency financial deep learning; time-series-toimage learning and visual feature baselines; explainable AI
and saliency-map limitations; decision-support systems and
dashboard artifacts; and the final research gap.
A. Financial Machine Learning and Chart-Image Prediction
Financial forecasting is a difficult domain for machine learning because the signal-to-noise ratio is low, labels are unstable,
and evaluation can be distorted by leakage, non-stationarity and
selection bias. Modern financial machine learning therefore
emphasizes careful data handling, chronological validation
and skepticism toward purely in-sample performance [2], [10],
[27]. Deep learning has expanded the model families available
for financial forecasting, including supervised forecasting and
reinforcement-learning approaches to trading and portfolio
management, but the literature continues to stress that predictive
performance should be interpreted cautiously, especially when
the task is short-horizon return direction [3], [4], [28]–[30].
A central strand of the literature studies whether visual representations of prices contain predictive information. Traditional

This thesis follows the core idea of Re-Imag but adapts it
to a different applied problem. The benchmark study operates
in a daily equity setting, while the present study works with
fifteen-minute MOEX Si futures data. The benchmark study is
oriented toward return prediction and portfolio evaluation, while
this thesis is oriented toward selective decision support and
analyst inspection. The difference is not incidental: it defines
the contribution. The present project asks whether chart-image
CNN logic can be transformed into an intraday futures decision-

support artifact that abstains from ambiguous cases and links
selected cases to raw chart and XAI evidence.
Financial time-series forecasting surveys provide further
context. Sezer, Gudelek and Ozbayoglu review deep learning
approaches in financial forecasting and show the importance of
CNNs, recurrent models and hybrid architectures in priceprediction applications [3]. Lim and Zohren review deep
learning for time-series forecasting more broadly, emphasizing
representation learning, sequence modeling, interpretability
and uncertainty [4]. These surveys support the methodological
choice to use deep visual models, but they also reinforce that
forecasting systems should be evaluated in relation to horizon,
data regime and decision use case rather than by one accuracy
score alone.
The literature therefore suggests a cautious principle: chartimage CNNs are a legitimate research direction, but their
practical value depends on the decision context, validation
design and interpretation layer. This principle directly motivates
the selective and explainable design of the present system.
B. Intraday and High-Frequency Financial Deep Learning
Intraday financial prediction differs from daily prediction
because short-horizon returns are affected by microstructure
effects, liquidity, order flow, intraday seasonality and marketimpact considerations. Market microstructure theory emphasizes that observed prices reflect the interaction of information,
liquidity provision and trading mechanisms [31]. For this
reason, intraday forecasting models often rely on more detailed
representations of market dynamics than daily models.
Deep learning has been widely applied to high-frequency and
limit-order-book data. DeepLOB is a prominent example, using

deep neural networks to forecast price movement from limitorder-book states [11]. Other studies use CNNs and recurrent
models to learn structure from order-book depth, order-flow
dynamics and high-frequency price formation [12], [13], [32].
Support-vector and other machine-learning approaches have
also been used to model limit-order-book dynamics [33]. These
studies show that deep models can extract structure from highdimensional intraday market data, but they also highlight the
complexity of evaluation under market microstructure noise.
The present thesis differs from limit-order-book prediction
because it does not use order-book depth or tick-level order flow.
Instead, it uses fifteen-minute OHLC chart images constructed
from Si futures candles. This is a coarser representation, but it is
more directly connected to trader-facing chart interpretation and
dashboard-based visual audit. The model therefore sits between
daily chart-image learning and high-frequency microstructure
modeling. It is intraday, but not order-book based.
This intermediate position has practical advantages and
limitations. The advantage is interpretability and operational
simplicity: chart images can be inspected by an analyst and
linked to familiar candlestick or price-path structures. The
limitation is that some microstructure information is lost. The
value/turnover field is unavailable in the supplied data because
it remains all zero, so the final system cannot claim to exploit
valid turnover or transaction-value information. This limitation
strengthens the case for caution and for the use of abstention
rather than universal prediction.
The intraday literature also supports the need for chronological validation. Random train/test splitting would be inappropriate because adjacent windows overlap and future information
could leak into training. The present project therefore uses
chronological data organization and treats overlapping images
as a source of dependence that must be discussed, not ignored.
C. Time-Series-to-Image Learning and Visual Feature Baselines
A broader time-series representation literature supports
the conversion of temporal data into images. Wang and
Oates proposed encoding time series as images, showing that
visual representations can enable CNN-based classification of
temporal patterns [14]. Related representations include Gramian
Angular Fields, Markov Transition Fields and recurrence
plots, all of which transform temporal relationships into twodimensional structures that can be processed by computervision models [34], [35]. In financial applications, Sezer and
Ozbayoglu show how time-series-to-image approaches can be
used for trading-related prediction tasks [36].
Deep learning for time-series classification is also relevant.
Fawaz et al. review deep neural networks for time-series
classification and show that CNN-style architectures are a
major family of models for learning temporal patterns [15].
InceptionTime and ROCKET represent two important directions
in time-series classification: deep convolutional ensembles and
large randomized convolutional feature transformations [16],
[17]. The hctsa framework and shapelet methods represent
another family of approaches based on large-scale feature

extraction or discriminative subsequences [37]–[40]. Distancebased methods such as dynamic time warping and DBA provide
additional baselines for comparing temporal shapes [41], [42].
The present thesis does not attempt to benchmark every timeseries classification method, nor does it use conditional visualreasoning layers such as FiLM; those architectures remain
adjacent background for future model extensions [43]. Instead,
it uses the literature to justify the representation choice and
to interpret visual baselines. The chart image is a direct
visual representation, not a transformed recurrence or Gramian
field. It is selected because the project is explicitly traderfacing: analysts can inspect candlestick-style chart images more
naturally than abstract time-series images.
Classical computer-vision features are used as important
comparators. HOG captures distributions of local gradient
orientations and is well suited for edge and shape information
[44]. HAAR-like features summarize contrast patterns over
rectangular regions and have a long history in computer vision
[45]. In the Re-Imag context, such classical visual features
are relevant because they test whether predictive information
can be extracted from chart geometry without a deep CNN.
In this thesis, the HOG tail baseline performs meaningfully,
reaching 0.704 accuracy, 0.754 balanced accuracy and 0.455
MCC at 2.0% coverage. HAAR-like features are weaker, with
the best reported row reaching 0.574 accuracy, 0.582 balanced
accuracy and 0.155 MCC at 5.1% coverage. This comparison
supports a balanced conclusion: visual structure matters, but the
strongest selective CNN result remains superior in the supplied
experiment package.
D. Explainable AI and Saliency-Map Limitations
Explainability is essential in this thesis because the proposed
artifact is intended for human-in-the-loop decision support. A
black-box score alone is insufficient for analyst trust, especially
in a financial domain where model errors can have operational
consequences. General XAI surveys define the field and
distinguish transparent models, post-hoc explanations, local
explanations and global explanations [18]–[20]. XAI in finance
is particularly relevant because financial applications require
accountability, risk awareness and practical interpretability [21].
For deep neural networks, visual explanation methods are
a major class of post-hoc interpretation. Class Activation
Mapping (CAM) and Grad-CAM highlight image regions
associated with class-discriminative decisions [5], [46]. GradCAM++ refines Grad-CAM for improved localization in some
settings [47]. Score-CAM, SmoothGrad, Layer-wise Relevance
Propagation, Integrated Gradients, DeepLIFT, LIME, SHAP,
counterfactual explanations, influence functions and TCAV
represent related or alternative approaches for interpreting blackbox models [48]–[57]. These methods vary in assumptions,
computational cost and explanation target.
However, the XAI literature also warns against overclaiming
saliency maps. Adebayo et al. show that some saliency
methods can fail sanity checks, meaning that visually plausible
explanations do not always reflect learned model parameters [6].
Lipton cautions that interpretability is often used imprecisely,

and Rudin argues that in high-stakes applications, interpretable
models should be preferred when possible rather than relying
uncritically on explanations of black boxes [7], [58]. Miller
emphasizes that human explanations are selective, contrastive
and social, not merely technical heatmaps [59]. These works
are critical for this thesis because they justify a cautious
interpretation of Grad-CAM.
The present project uses Grad-CAM as an audit layer, not
as proof of causal reasoning. Initial analysis of heatmaponly images suggested broad or possibly background-heavy
activation. Later masked-overlay, full-overlay and raw selectedcase images refined the interpretation: in representative selected
cases, activation is generally chart-anchored but spatially diffuse.
This means the model is not simply focusing on empty
background, but the highlighted regions extend beyond the
precise candle path. Accordingly, Grad-CAM is interpreted as
support for pattern-level inspection and diagnostic review, not
as evidence of exact candle-level causal explanation.
A closely related applied precedent is the KOSPI 200 futures
CNN and Grad-CAM study, which is useful because it connects
futures prediction, CNNs, chart-style data and Grad-CAM-style
visualization [60]. The present thesis differs by emphasizing
selective abstention, dashboard workflow, and MOEX Si futures
decision support.

These components make the artifact practical and academically
defensible.
This decision-support framing also protects the thesis from
overclaiming. The dashboard does not present the CNN as a
fully automated trading agent. Instead, it supports analyst triage.
A surfaced case is an object for review, not a command to trade.
This is consistent with the empirical results: the full-sample
model edge is modest, while selected tail cases are stronger
but limited in coverage.
F. Research Gap and Positioning

The reviewed literature establishes five points. First, financial
machine learning can extract patterns from market data, but
evaluation must be cautious because of noise, leakage and overfitting risk. Second, chart-image CNNs are a strong and relevant
methodological direction, especially after “(Re-)Imag(in)ing
Price Trends.” Third, intraday forecasting is more difficult
than daily chart prediction because of microstructure noise,
overlapping windows and short-horizon instability. Fourth,
XAI can support audit and communication but cannot prove
causal reasoning by itself. Fifth, project-based decision-support
artifacts require more than model accuracy: they require
workflow, governance and human inspection.

C. Data Provenance and Continuous-Front Construction
The empirical basis of the study is a package of MOEX Si
futures data and derived artifacts. The raw data contain 775,621
one-minute rows across multiple futures contracts. These are
aggregated into 89,628 fifteen-minute by-contract candles. A
clean continuous-front series is then constructed, resulting in
29,926 fifteen-minute observations over 502 clean trading dates.
The front-contract sequence used in the project is:
SiH4 → SiM4 → SiU4 → SiZ4
→ SiH5 → SiM5 → SiU5 → SiZ5.
The continuous-front construction is necessary because the
purpose of the project is to build a single chronological intraday
decision-support workflow rather than a separate model for
each isolated contract. Contract rolls are therefore treated as
part of the data-engineering problem. Table I summarizes the
data lineage, while Table II reports the continuous-front roll
schedule used to construct the chronological futures series.
The reproducibility package contains 51 files: 21 result files, 8
model files, 7 derived-data files and 15 raw-data files.
A key data limitation is that the value or turnover column
remains zero in the raw, fifteen-minute and clean-front tables.
It is therefore not used as a valid predictive feature. This is
important because some chart-image methods include volume
or trading activity panels. In the present project, any feature
relying directly on the unavailable value field must be treated
as absent or as a limitation, not as a measured source of
information.

I. Grad-CAM and XAI Diagnostics
The XAI component uses Grad-CAM-style visual explanations to inspect where the CNN places discriminative emphasis
in chart images [5]. Grad-CAM produces a class-discriminative
heatmap over the image, which can be rendered as a heatmaponly image, a full overlay, or a masked overlay. The thesis
uses these views as an audit mechanism rather than as proof
of causal reasoning.
The XAI analysis has two levels. The first level is quantitative allocation, where heat is compared against foreground/background or chart/volume masks. Under strict
foreground-mask definitions, the selected cases can appear
background-focused. For example, the strict-best Option B
Grad-CAM summary reports 54 selected rows with average
dilated foreground heat of 0.142 and average background heat
of 0.858. This strict-mask result should be reported as a caution
signal.
The second level is visual case audit. Later image batches
include heatmap-only, full-overlay, masked-overlay and selected
raw images for representative cases. These images refine the
interpretation. The selected examples are not purely backgroundfocused: the activations are usually anchored around visible
chart structures, trend segments, turning areas and late-window

breaks. However, the highlighted regions are broad and often
extend beyond the exact candle trace.
The final XAI interpretation is therefore:
Visual inspection of Grad-CAM outputs suggests that
the CNN often attends to chart foreground structures
and major trend segments, but the highlighted regions
are typically broad and extend beyond the immediate candle path. Accordingly, Grad-CAM is most
useful here as a diagnostic and decision-audit tool
rather than as definitive proof of precise candle-level
reasoning.
This wording is intentionally cautious and is consistent with
the broader XAI literature, which warns that saliency maps
can be visually appealing but incomplete measures of model
reasoning [6], [7], [20].

J. Evaluation Metrics
Because the class balance and selected-case coverage vary
across experiments, raw accuracy alone is insufficient. The
evaluation therefore reports accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC),
ROC-AUC where available, coverage and row count.
Balanced accuracy is used because it averages sensitivity
across classes. F1-score summarizes precision and recall. MCC
is especially useful in binary classification because it uses
all four confusion-matrix cells and remains informative when
classes or predictions are imbalanced. ROC-AUC is reported
for full-sample probabilistic models when available, but it is not
always meaningful for selected tail subsets if only thresholded
selected rows are evaluated.
Accordingly, each selective result is reported with retained
coverage and selected row count. In particular, the strictfinalist result is reported as “0.833 accuracy at 2.0% coverage,
corresponding to 54 selected rows.” This prevents the reader
from confusing a small, high-confidence subset with the full
test distribution.
In summary, the methodology can be summarized as
follows. First, raw one-minute MOEX Si futures data are
aggregated to fifteen-minute candles and converted into a
clean continuous-front series. Second, rolling chart images
are generated and labeled by future return direction. Third,
several CNN and baseline models are trained and evaluated
under chronological splitting. Fourth, instead of deploying
an always-on classifier, the system applies a probability-tail
selection rule to retain only high-confidence cases. Fifth, GradCAM and raw/overlay/masked images are used to audit selected
decisions. Finally, the selected cases are integrated into the
Decision Support Studio dashboard for analyst inspection.
This methodology directly supports the project-based thesis
objective: to design and evaluate a selective, explainable and
human-in-the-loop decision-support artifact for intraday futures
trading analysis.
IV. Solution Architecture and Decision-Support Artifact
This section describes the proposed practical solution: an
explainable, selective and human-in-the-loop decision-support

A. System Architecture
The system architecture follows the complete evidence chain
from raw market observations to analyst-facing decisions. The
architecture consists of seven layers:
1) raw MOEX Si futures one-minute data;
2) fifteen-minute by-contract OHLC aggregation;
3) clean continuous-front construction;
4) rolling chart-image generation;
5) CNN and baseline model scoring;
6) selective probability-tail filtering;
7) Grad-CAM visual audit and dashboard presentation.
This architecture is shown conceptually in Figure 1. The
system starts from 775,621 raw one-minute rows and produces
a clean continuous-front fifteen-minute series with 29,926 rows
over 502 trading dates. From this series, rolling chart-image
samples are constructed. Model predictions are generated for
eligible samples, but only high-confidence probability-tail cases
are surfaced for analyst review. The selected cases are linked
to raw images, heatmaps, full overlays, masked overlays, labels,
predicted probabilities, correctness indicators and forward
returns.
The architecture reflects a design-science view of the project.
The artifact is not only the CNN model; it is the integrated
decision process that includes data engineering, model scoring,
abstention, explainability and user-facing inspection. This
is consistent with design-science research principles, which
evaluate artifacts by their ability to solve an applied problem
in a relevant environment [22]–[24].
B. Data-to-Decision Pipeline
The data-to-decision pipeline converts raw market data into
a structured analyst queue. Its stages are as follows.
First, one-minute futures records are aggregated to fifteenminute OHLC candles for each contract. Second, a continuousfront series is constructed using the front-contract roll sequence:
SiH4 → SiM4 → SiU4 → SiZ4
→ SiH5 → SiM5 → SiU5 → SiZ5.

Third, rolling windows are converted into candlestick or chart
images. Fourth, each image is scored by the CNN and, where
relevant, by baseline models. Fifth, the selective tail rule keeps
only cases whose predicted probability falls into the most
extreme low or high probability regions. Sixth, Grad-CAM
visualizations are generated for selected cases. Seventh, the
selected cases and their visual evidence are loaded into the
dashboard.
The pipeline deliberately separates prediction from recommendation. A probability score is not automatically a
recommendation. A case becomes decision-support material
only when it passes the selective rule and is visible in the
dashboard with sufficient evidence for analyst inspection. This
separation is important because the full-sample model edge is
modest, whereas the selective tail cases are stronger but based
on a small selected sample. The pipeline therefore reduces the
risk of presenting ordinary ambiguous windows as actionable
signals.

C. Model and Selective-Rule Integration
The dashboard integrates model outputs at two levels. At
the first level, it shows aggregate model evidence: full-sample
performance, selective frontier performance and strict-finalist
performance. At the second level, it shows case-level evidence:
probability, predicted direction, true label when available,
forward return, signal bucket, confidence bucket and XAI
quality bucket.
The core integration logic is the selective probability-tail
rule. For a predicted probability 𝑝 𝑡 , extreme low values indicate
high-confidence predicted-down cases and extreme high values
indicate high-confidence predicted-up cases. The system retains
both sides symmetrically. This is important because a decisionsupport queue supports both long/up and short/down analytical
hypotheses. In the Grad-CAM selected export, the 54 selected
rows are balanced by predicted side: 27 predicted-up and 27
predicted-down cases.
Consistent with selective prediction and related reject-option
or abstention logic [61]–[63], the strongest canonical selected result comes from single_I120_ma0_vol0, which reaches
0.833 accuracy at 2.0% coverage, corresponding to 54 selected
rows. This result is displayed as the strict finalist rather than
as a general test-set accuracy. The dashboard also includes
broader selective evidence, such as the I60 rule around 5.1% or
10.1% coverage, because an analyst may prefer a larger review
queue with lower precision depending on operational needs.
The integration design therefore supports three operating
modes:
1) Full-sample monitoring: use aggregate metrics to understand the model’s general edge.
2) Selective triage: use probability-tail filtering to surface
high-confidence cases.
3) Case audit: inspect the raw chart and XAI views before
treating a case as decision support.

D. Grad-CAM Image Audit Workflow
The Grad-CAM audit workflow links each selected case to
four visual views:
1) raw chart image;
2) heatmap-only Grad-CAM image;
3) full overlay image;
4) masked overlay image.
The raw image shows the actual CNN input. The heatmaponly image shows the spatial distribution of class-discriminative
activation. The full overlay shows the heatmap superimposed
on the raw chart, making it possible to see whether activation
is near visible chart structures. The masked overlay retains
the visually emphasized chart regions and suppresses the rest,
helping the analyst identify the chart geometry associated with
the selected case.
This workflow is necessary because the strict quantitative
mask and visual inspection do not always give the same
impression. Under strict foreground/background masks, selected
cases can appear background-heavy. However, when raw,
overlay and masked views are inspected together, representative
selected cases are better described as chart-anchored but
spatially diffuse. The highlighted regions often align with major
trend segments, local turning regions or late-window breaks,
but they are not sharply restricted to individual candle pixels.
The case-level audit supports three interpretation rules:
1) if activation is chart-anchored and the probability is
extreme, the case may be suitable for analyst review;
2) if activation is diffuse or background-heavy under the
strict mask, the case should be treated cautiously;
3) Grad-CAM is never interpreted as proof of causal market
reasoning.
This is consistent with the XAI literature, which treats
saliency methods as post-hoc diagnostic tools rather than
definitive explanations [5]–[7], [20].

F. Analyst Workflow and Operating Procedure
The proposed operating procedure has six steps.

1) Review system context. The analyst first observes the
global KPI cards and confirms whether the model branch,
coverage level and evidence hierarchy are appropriate for
the current analysis session.
2) Filter the queue. The analyst filters by contract, signal
direction, confidence, XAI group, date or search term.
3) Inspect probability and coverage. The analyst checks
whether the case belongs to a strict high-confidence
subset or a broader selective subset.
4) Inspect chart evidence. The analyst compares the raw
chart with the heatmap-only, full-overlay and maskedoverlay views.
5) Apply caution rules. If the case is diffuse, backgroundheavy by strict mask or inconsistent with visible chart
structure, the analyst treats it as lower confidence.
6) Record decision-support interpretation. The case
can be documented as supportive evidence, ambiguous

evidence or rejected evidence, but it should not be
executed automatically as a trade.
This workflow converts a machine-learning score into a
governed analytical process. It also aligns with the thesis’s
practical management goal: to support decision quality under
uncertainty by providing a structured, auditable and cautious
signal-review procedure.

V. Results, Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion
This chapter evaluates whether the proposed system solves
the project problem formulated in the previous chapters:
transforming noisy intraday futures prediction into a selective,
auditable and human-in-the-loop decision-support workflow.
The evidence is presented in a deliberately conservative order.

the original always-on framing was insufficient. Second, the

proof of automated trading readiness.

question of whether the model is focusing on the chart or the
background. In the representative selected cases, the answer
is not purely background-focused; it is chart-anchored but
spatially diffuse.
I. Decision Support Studio Artifact
The final dashboard, named Decision Support Studio, operationalizes the project contribution. It contains 404 curated
rows, including 54 selected prediction rows linked to GradCAM/dashboard evidence. It supports raw chart, full overlay,
masked overlay and heatmap-only views. It also includes filters
by contract, split, signal, confidence, XAI quality, date and
search term.
The dashboard displays KPI cards, a signal queue, a caselevel decision studio, a benchmark ladder, a selective coverage
frontier, XAI foreground/background diagnostics, queue composition, operational stance and an evidence hierarchy. These
components are not cosmetic. They implement the management
logic of the project: the analyst is shown what the model

predicts, how confident the model is, how selective the rule is,
and what visual evidence is available for inspection.
The dashboard operationalizes the proposed decision-support
framework by connecting selected CNN probability-tail cases
to raw chart images, Grad-CAM heatmaps, masked overlays,
confidence buckets, and coverage-performance analytics. Its
purpose is not to automate trades, but to provide an auditable
queue for analyst inspection.
The artifact contribution is important because it converts
model outputs into an operational analyst workflow with
governance rules and caution messages. It shows a proposed
solution, not only an empirical analysis.
J. Discussion of Findings
The results support four main findings.
First, the full-sample edge is modest. The best full-sample
CNN reaches 0.587 accuracy and 0.580 ROC-AUC. This is
better than the initial 20-bar result and better than simple
numeric benchmarks, but it is not strong enough to justify
universal prediction. This finding is important because it
prevents the thesis from overclaiming.
Second, selective prediction substantially improves decision
relevance. The strict I120 finalist achieves 0.833 accuracy at
2.0% coverage, corresponding to 54 selected rows. The broader
I60 selective rule also achieves approximately 0.693 accuracy
at about 10.1% coverage. These results show that confidencetail filtering can turn a weak full-sample signal into a more
useful decision-support queue.
Third, visual baselines matter. The HOG baseline performs
meaningfully at 2.0% coverage, with 0.704 accuracy and
0.455 MCC. This confirms that chart-image structure contains exploitable visual information, while the stronger CNN
strict finalist suggests that learned CNN probabilities provide
additional selective value.
Fourth, Grad-CAM is useful but limited. It provides an audit
trail that helps analysts inspect selected cases, but it does not
prove causal reasoning or exact candle-level understanding. The
correct XAI conclusion is chart-anchored but spatially diffuse.
Together, these findings support the central contribution: an
intraday futures decision-support artifact that combines chartimage CNN prediction, selective abstention, visual explanation
and dashboard-based analyst workflow.
K. Limitations
Several limitations must be stated clearly.
First, the strongest result is based on a small selected sample.
The strict finalist covers only 2.0% of cases and contains 54
selected rows. A Wilson binomial interval for 45/54 gives
an approximate 95% confidence interval of [0.713, 0.910].
This interval is interpreted cautiously because rolling-window
dependence can reduce the effective sample size. The result
is suitable for selective decision support, but it cannot be
generalized as full-sample predictive accuracy.
Second, the selected Grad-CAM export is contractconcentrated. It contains 53 SiZ5 rows and one SiU5 row.

Therefore, the image-level XAI evidence is not used to claim
broad cross-contract generalization.
Third, the dashboard/Grad-CAM recomputation gives 46/54
correct while the formal grid reports 45/54. The manuscript
uses the grid result as canonical and treats the dashboard value
as an export consistency note.
Fourth, Grad-CAM is post-hoc and imperfect. It can indicate
visual regions associated with a prediction, but it does not
establish causal market reasoning. Saliency methods can be
sensitive to architecture, preprocessing and visualization choices
[6], [18], [20].
Fifth, the value/turnover field is unavailable because it
remains all zero in the supplied raw and derived data. Therefore,
the study does not claim to exploit valid turnover information.
Sixth, rolling-window samples are overlapping and serially
dependent. Adjacent images can be visually similar while
labels change, especially around turning points. Chronological
splitting mitigates leakage risk, but the effective independence
of samples is lower than the raw row count suggests.
Seventh, the study does not establish a final transactioncost-adjusted profitable trading strategy. No final confirmed
Sharpe ratio, Sortino ratio, drawdown or cost-sensitive backtest
package is available in the supplied final facts. Consequently,
profitability is reserved for future work.
L. Future Work
Future research can extend the project in five directions.
First, the selective signals can be evaluated in a transactioncost-aware economic backtest, including turnover, slippage,
drawdown and risk-adjusted return. Second, the model can
be tested across additional contracts and market regimes to
examine generalization beyond the SiZ5-concentrated selected
export. Third, XAI robustness can be evaluated with alternative
methods such as Score-CAM, Integrated Gradients, SmoothGrad and saliency sanity checks [6], [48], [49], [51]. Fourth,
selective prediction could be formalized further with calibrated
abstention and selective-classification methods [61]–[63]. Fifth,
the dashboard artifact can be evaluated with analyst users to
measure whether the explanation and queue design improve
decision quality.
M. Conclusion
This study developed and evaluated an explainable selective
decision-support artifact for intraday MOEX Si futures analysis.
Building on the chart-image CNN logic of “(Re-)Imag(in)ing
Price Trends,” the project adapted visual price representations
to a fifteen-minute futures setting, expanded the model design
with longer and multi-scale intraday inputs, compared CNNs
with numeric and classical visual baselines, applied selective
probability-tail rules and integrated Grad-CAM evidence into
a dashboard workflow.
